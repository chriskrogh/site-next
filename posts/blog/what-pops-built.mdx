I can still picture Pops behind the wheel of his pickup truck, driving through Haeland Park like the speed bumps weren't there.

<br />

The rest of us bounced around inside. Chris Cupp was in the back seat, and one good bump sent him high enough to hit his head on the roof. I can’t remember where we were going. What I remember is the truck, the bump, and the laughter.

<br />

That's how so many memories of Pops come back to me: not as big, important occasions, but as little scenes that still feel alive in my mind.

<br />

I remember him picking us up from our swim meets at Marlins to go get lunch. We would pile in, tired and hungry, and he would ask how we did. He genuinely wanted to know. He listened to our stories before telling a few of his own, and Pops always had a story.

<br />

He had 8 kids and 32 grandkids. On paper, I was one of many. But when I was sitting with him, I never felt like one of 32. I felt like I was the only one.

<br />

That was one of his gifts. Somehow, his family could keep growing without his attention ever feeling divided. Whether you were a baby, a child, a teenager, or an adult, Pops met you where you were. He made you feel interesting. He made you feel loved.

<br />

After he heard what was happening in my life, it was usually his turn. He would tell us about something he had built years ago in an oilfield or at a manufacturing plant. There was the hoist he pioneered to move plastic powder across a factory floor, or the vertical tank storage solution he designed so people could “pull from the bottom” and get the job done so much faster.

<br />

I didn’t always understand the mechanics. I understood the way he told the stories. There was a problem, other people doubted it could be solved, and Pops found a way.

<br />

When I moved away to Canada for university, I heard those stories less often, and they began to mean so much more to me. Distance has a way of teaching you the value of ordinary moments. What had once felt like another familiar Pops story became a chance to sit with him, hear his voice, and carry a little more of him with me when I left.

<br />

Pops fired from the hip and got things done. If he started working on something, it was going to get finished, even if the finished product was held together with whatever happened to be within reach.

<br />

There was a drawer in the house with a missing handle. Most people would have gone to the store and bought a replacement. Pops found a LEGO piece, some super glue, and a single screw. Problem solved.

<br />

It may not have been elegant, but it worked. And every time I think about it, I smile because it was so completely Pops.

<br />

His workshop wasn’t the only place where that spirit showed up. I remember being about six years old and watching him fix something in the yard with a hammer. He missed whatever he was aiming for, hit his finger, and started to cuss.

<br />

From inside the kitchen, Granny immediately called out, “Don’t curse around the kids!”

<br />

Even now, I can picture the whole thing. Pops in the yard with the hammer. Granny keeping him honest from the kitchen. Me standing nearby, trying to make sense of the grown-ups.

<br />

Pops and Granny had a life full of moments like that. More than anything else, Pops loved his wife. Together, they showed all of us what a long and happy marriage could look like.

<br />

I can only hope that my own marriage will be as long, as full, and as happy as theirs.

<br />

When I got married, Pops made it to our wedding with a cane and a smile. Seeing him there meant the world to me. I was so happy that he got to share that day with us.

<br />

Pops built for his grandkids too. He made us box carts to race down the hills of Haeland Park and stilts to stumble around on together. I’m sure those projects began the same way so many of his projects did: with an idea, whatever materials he could find, and the confidence that he would figure out the rest along the way.

<br />

At the time, they were just toys. We hopped in and took off down the hill. We didn’t think about the hours he had spent making them.

<br />

I think about those hours now.

<br />

I think about all the ways Pops spent his life building. He built machines that solved problems people thought could not be solved. He built box carts and stilts that made childhood feel like an adventure. He built a marriage that gave the rest of us something to aspire to. He built a family so large that it should have been easy to feel lost inside it, yet he made each of us feel seen.

<br />

That may be the lesson I will carry with me most. Love is not only something you feel. It's something you build.

<br />

You build it by showing up at a swim meet and taking the kids to lunch. You build it by asking someone what is new and caring about the answer. You build it with stories told so many times that they become part of the family.

<br />

And you build it by giving people your time. Whether you have one grandchild or 32.

<br />

I hope I carry some of Pops with me: his resourcefulness, his willingness to act, his humor, and his determination to finish what he started. But more than any of that, I hope I can make the people I love feel the way he made me feel.

<br />

Not like one of many.

<br />

Like the only one.

<br />

I miss you, Pops.

<br />

Thank you for everything you built.
